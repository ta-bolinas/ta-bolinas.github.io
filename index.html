About

Tawny A. Bolinas

Hi! I’m a scientist and educator in the biological sciences. I teach biology as Associate Faculty at Butte College and serve on the board of the Friends of the Ahart Herbarium.

I started at Cuesta College, then earned a B.S. in Cellular and Molecular Biology and an M.S. in Microbial Ecology at California State University, Chico. My thesis examined how foliar fungal endophytes influence microbial community assembly and leaf litter decomposition.

In the classroom and lab, I focus on hands-on learning and accessible, student-centered science education. Through fellowships, university service, and outreach, I’ve also worked in science communication, mentoring, and lab management. I’m interested in using molecular and computational approaches to understand how microbial communities shape ecosystem function.

Priority Effects

M.S. thesis · CSU Chico · 2023–2025 · Advisor: Dr. Gerald M. Cobián

2026Outstanding Master’s Thesis Award, Office of Graduate Studies

Some foliar endophytic fungi may stay dormant in leaves until senescence, then begin decomposition and, through priority effects, shape the decomposer fungi that arrive later. I tested this with a 36-week field experiment using valley oak (Quercus lobata) litter in mesh litterbags assigned to three treatments: endophytes only, external fungi, and both combined. Fungal communities were tracked with ITS high-throughput sequencing, yielding 515 OTUs at 97% similarity, mostly Ascomycota and Basidiomycota.

Community composition was shaped mainly by site, with species turnover driving most differences, and decomposition rates did not differ among treatments. Indicator species analysis identified distinct OTUs for each treatment. Endophytes imposed weak priority effects under field conditions, and long-term community assembly was more strongly shaped by site-specific stochastic processes.

Endophytes to Decomposers

Undergraduate research · CSU Chico · 2020–2022 · Advisor: Dr. Gerald M. Cobián

Foliar fungal endophytes colonize plant leaves without obvious harm to the host, and many are thought to be commensals that wait for leaves to senesce. Fungi are the primary decomposers of leaf litter, but how much endophyte and decay communities overlap is not well understood. To measure that overlap, fungal DNA was extracted from surface-sterilized canyon live oak (Quercus chrysolepis) leaves and litter at Big Chico Creek Ecological Reserve, and both communities were characterized with high-throughput amplicon sequencing and compared using alpha and beta diversity.

Endophyte and decay communities differed significantly, with fewer than 25% of taxa occurring in both substrates. This partial overlap suggests that some foliar endophytes act as early colonizers and may influence the composition of leaf decay communities.

Other research experience

Optimizing fungal DNA extraction

2024 · Adelante Summer Research · PI: Gerald M. Cobián

Compared lysing bead matrices (F, J, I) and buffer volumes for extracting endophyte DNA from valley oak leaves. Matrix F with 350 µL of buffer, increased to 400 µL, each step with 2 minutes of homogenization, gave the strongest PCR amplification.

Abstract Article

Eliminating foliar endophytes

2023 · Adelante Summer Research · PI: Gerald M. Cobián

With no irradiation facilities nearby, tested autoclave time and temperature combinations for sterilizing valley oak leaves. 75 °C for 15 minutes was the lowest treatment that fully eliminated foliar endophytes, setting the method for the thesis field experiment.

Wastewater surveillance

2021–2022 · PIs: Troy Cline, Gerald M. Cobián

Stem cell–conditioned media

2022 · PI: David Stachura

Double-blind trial in mice testing stem cell–conditioned media, PGE2, minoxidil, and redensyl for androgenetic alopecia. All treatments accelerated hair growth in preliminary results, and combined factors worked best.

Outreach

Friends of the Ahart Herbarium

Board Member · 2026–present

The Friends of the Ahart Herbarium supports the Ahart Herbarium at Chico State, the most complete collection of plant specimens from northeastern California, with more than 100,000 specimens of vascular plants, bryophytes, lichens, and slime molds. The group promotes research, education, and preservation of Northern California’s native plant diversity through workshops, events, and its annual meeting.
